# Framework source: principles → rules

Derived from Kenneth Andrade (founder, Old Bridge Capital; ex-CIO IDFC AMC) on *Conversation with Kushal* (~2025). Each principle is paraphrased from the interview and mapped to the rule(s) that test it, so anyone can check that the score reflects what he said rather than generic value-investing ideas.

| # | Principle (paraphrased from the interview) | Rule(s) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "We buy capital efficiency at a price." The business is capital efficiency at a price, not banks, consumer or commodities. Sector labels don't matter. | Whole model; A + B pillars |
| 2 | A P/E is an earnings yield: 10x = 10% yield, 15x ≈ 6.5–7%. Compare it with what other asset classes pay (if the bond index yields 10%, equity should yield close to that, plus growth because inflation is built in). | A1, A3 |
| 3 | Judge value on an absolute basis. Never call 30x "cheap" because the FMCG index trades at 50x. Compare across sectors: a consumer name against a commodity name against a bank. | A1 (absolute hurdle, not sector-relative) |
| 4 | Payback thinking: steel at a 2-year payback and power utilities at 3.5 years (≈30% yield) in 2018, while consumer names and private banks yielded about 2%. | A2 (FCF yield, payback years reported) |
| 5 | If the price moves faster than earnings, you are "buying too much of the future in the present". Few people can see 2 years ahead, let alone 10. | E4, G3, S5 |
| 6 | When the narrative and consensus are very strong, you are making a mistake. Be first into the cycle and leave money on the table for the next investor. | E3, E1 |
| 7 | Prefer free cash flow and no need for external funding. Capex should be internally funded. | A2, B2, C2, C3, C4 |
| 8 | Debt-free. When industry profitability shrinks, the company with the most debt has the biggest problem. Down-cycles raise inventories and receivables, so cash gets squeezed. | C1, G2, S6 |
| 9 | Buy the largest company in an industry that is not doing well. A small-cap in a weak industry is a falling knife. | D1, E1, E2 |
| 10 | The constant across every holding: incremental market share plus pricing power (Page Industries, MRF: dominant, monopolistic traits). | D2, D3, D4 |
| 11 | In a distressed industry, the bigger players get bigger. Consolidation is the opportunity. | E2 |
| 12 | Industry and company move together. If industry profitability is rising, every player gains (the smallest fastest). If it is collapsing, the smallest die. To be contrarian, find an industry whose profitability is stagnant (not falling) and the business consolidating it. | E1, E2 |
| 13 | The capital cycle as sell discipline: a 30% ROE draws in incumbents expanding, outsiders (20% hurdle), PE (15%) and banks (9%), and oversupply follows. A large conglomerate entering (cables, paints, broadband) means hyper-competition, lost pricing power, lost profitability and lost market cap. | G4, S1, S2, S7 |
| 14 | Lenders: there is no free cash flow and the balance sheet has to grow every year, so buy only at a reasonably-to-very attractive valuation (NBFCs in 2011 traded around 0.7–1.3x book). | Lender model: LA2, LA3, gates |
| 15 | New-age, EV and renewable businesses with no cash flow can't be priced. "Hope is a very bad investment strategy." Wait for cash flows and buy in the second cycle. | G1 |
| 16 | Peak capex behind, cash flows near all-time highs, debt no longer a crisis (his metals and pharma thesis). | C2, C3, C1 |
| 17 | Don't go by hearsay about promoters. In a down-cycle every promoter looks like a capital misallocator. Judge on the numbers. | No subjective governance score by design |
| 18 | Exit quickly when a trade isn't working, even at the bottom. Don't over-analyse. | S-triggers → REVIEW / EXIT |
| 19 | About 20–25 stocks, concentrated, each the most efficient player in its network. | Use ranking mode across candidates |

## Design choices (and why)

- **Hurdle = risk-free/bond yield.** He anchors on "the bond index". In India use the 10-year G-sec yield as the default, or a AAA corporate bond index for a stricter test. Always record the value and its date.
- **Latest PAT for the earnings yield, not normalised.** It mirrors his metals comment ("13–20x earnings when commodities have no pricing power") and avoids optimistic normalisation. Cyclicals at a trough get penalised in A1 but rewarded in E1/E2. That tension is intended: it is exactly the contrarian set-up he describes.
- **Market share is relative change** (e.g. 26% → 28.4% = +9.2%), so large and small players are comparable.
- **Industry ROE** = median ROE of the top listed peers (or an industry aggregate) over at least 5 years, ideally 10. It drives the cycle tests, so source it carefully.
- **Absolute price before relative price.** A3, E3 and E4 score only if EY ≥ 0.5× hurdle. G3 caps the score at 60 when EY < hurdle/2 and at 40 when EY < hurdle/3. This was added after validating on Page Industries (Nov 2026: 54x P/E, EY 1.85% vs 7.15% G-sec). Without it, Page scored 78 "CORE FIT" purely on being cheap against its own 70x history, which contradicts principle 3.
- **Share gain must be current.** D2/E2 give no credit if share fell in each of the last 2 years, even if it is up over the full 5-year window. Page's share rose FY22→FY23 and then fell for three years.
- **No qualitative overrides inside the score.** Judgement goes in the narrative, never in the points, so the number stays reproducible.
